## How does the airplane generate lift?

Lift is the aerodynamic force perpendicular to the airflow. As the airplane moves through the air, its wings deflect air and establish a pressure distribution that produces lift. The angle between the wing's chord line and the relative airflow, called the angle of attack, strongly affects lift.

The lift equation is L = 1/2 $\rho$ v^2 S Cl

A larger wing area can generate the required lift at a lower speed, provided the wing remains at a suitable angle of attack and does not stall.

## Lift, weight, drag, and thrust

| Flight phase | Forces and behaviour                                                                                       |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Launch       | The hand supplies the initial velocity and energy. Lift develops as air flows over the wings.              |
| Glide        | Lift approximately balances weight perpendicular to the flight path, while drag opposes motion.            |
| Descent      | Gravity supplies the energy needed to overcome aerodynamic drag, so the aircraft gradually loses altitude. |
| Landing      | Airspeed decreases and lift falls until the aircraft settles onto the surface.                             |

A paper airplane has no sustained propulsion. After release, it converts gravitational potential energy into forward motion and aerodynamic work. Its thrust is effectively zero during the unpowered glide, apart from any brief effects immediately after the hand releases it.

## Wing area, wing loading, centre of gravity, and stability

Wing area: The total planform area of both wings. A larger wing area can help an airplane generate lift at lower speed.

Wing loading: The airplane's weight divided by its wing area.

$\text{Wing loading}=\frac{W}{S}$ 

Lower wing loading generally supports slower flight, which can help a glider stay aloft longer. However, wing shape, drag, and stability also matter.

Centre of gravity (CG): The point where the airplane's mass can be considered concentrated. If the CG is too far forward, the plane may dive; if it is too far backward, the plane may become unstable or stall repeatedly.

Stability: A stable airplane tends to return toward its normal flight attitude after a small disturbance. Symmetrical wings, a suitable CG, and slight dihedral can help the model glide predictably.

## Why might this design stay airborne longer than a conventional dart?

A broad-wing glider can operate at lower airspeed and lower wing loading than a typical narrow dart. If its wings generate adequate lift with relatively little drag, it can descend more slowly and remain airborne longer.

The important distinction is:

- Minimum sink rate: The lowest rate of altitude loss, measured in metres per second. This is the main target for maximum time aloft.
- Maximum glide distance: The greatest horizontal distance covered per unit of altitude lost. This depends primarily on the glide ratio, \(L/D\).

A design that travels far is not necessarily the one that stays airborne longest. For this challenge, I would prioritize a gentle, stable glide and low sink rate rather than maximum forward speed.
